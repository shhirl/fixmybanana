How it's built

A fun handstand detector, built like a real one

fixmybanana.com is a fun little tool with a real model behind it: upload a handstand photo and an OpenAI vision model tells you whether you have "good form" or a "banana back", the arched spine every beginner has.

It was one of my first fun projects with AI, built the way I learn best: by making something and seeing where it breaks. The subject wasn't random. Before data science I was a personal trainer, and I still spend a lot of my time training friends and keeping them accountable. A tool that tells you, kindly and with a bit of humour, that your handstand is a banana is the kind of thing I'd want in my own gym bag.

Then I asked the question I ask clients: how do I know it works? This page is my answer, kept as a lab notebook. Each version below shows what I changed, what it cost, and how it scored against the same fixed set of {{ c.n if c else 54 }} photos that I labelled by hand. Some of the numbers are bad. That's the point: you can't improve what you haven't measured, and I'd rather show you the measuring than the marketing.

Code, prompts, test set and every result CSV: github.com/shhirl/fixmybanana.

1 · THE PROBLEMA confident answer with nothing behind it

The first version did what most AI demos do: it sent a photo to a model with a prompt and showed whatever came back, in a nice card, as if it were a verdict. Five things were wrong with that, and none of them showed on the screen.

  • Nobody knew if it was right. Not me, not the user. There was no reference to compare against, so "it seems to work" was the only evidence.
  • The same photo could get different answers. Nothing measured how often, so a user who uploaded twice and got two verdicts had no way to know which to trust.
  • It had no way to say "I can't tell". Front views, mirrors, a plank, a statue: everything got a label or a broken message.
  • It could fail silently. When the account ran out of credits, the site kept returning 200 with an error card, and the code threw away the reason.
  • Cost was a feeling, not a number. "It's cheap" is not a budget.

The question this page answers is the one I'd ask a client about any model in production: how do you know, and what does it cost to find out?

4 · WHAT I TOOK AWAYFive things I'd carry into any AI build

  1. Measure before you improve, because the eval reorders the roadmap. Before the numbers, the obvious next step was a better prompt. After them, the biggest failure was an interface bug: a five-token limit turning a polite refusal into a broken verdict on 42 of 235 runs. No prompt tuning fixes that; a schema does. The eval cost $1.24 and changed what I'd build next.
  2. The label is a design decision, and it has its own error bar. A binary "banana or not" felt obvious and turned out to be the noisiest part of the system: two careful labellers agreed on 66% of photos, kappa 0.32. A model can't beat the target's own noise. Ask for a score with a tolerance band, and publish the inter-rater number as the ceiling.
  3. Non-determinism is something to measure, not hide. Temperature 0 still gave different answers to the same photo on 17% of the set. Running five times costs cents and turns a surprise into a signal: the spread marks the photos the model is unsure about even though it never says so. In production, that is the cheapest confidence estimate you can get.
  4. Give the model a way out. Most of the failures were the absence of "I can't tell": front views, a statue, a plank, all forced into a yes or no. A usable-photo flag and a confidence field with a reason remove a whole class of errors by construction, and they make the product feel honest instead of confident.
  5. Make failures say why, and treat cost as a stock, not just a rate. The site was silently dead because a prepaid balance hit zero and the code discarded the error body. Caps limit spend rate; a balance runs out. Log the status and body of every failed call, distinguish "out of quota" from "rate-limited", and check the model path end to end, because a home page returning 200 proves nothing.

How the tool works

One upload triggers up to two calls to OpenAI's chat-completions API, both with the photo attached.

  1. Classifier. Temperature 0, five output tokens, two text-only examples, told to answer exactly "good form" or "banana back". The app then checks whether the word "banana" or "good" appears in the reply; anything else is shown to the user raw, as "unclear".
  2. Coaching feedback. Only if the classifier said banana back: temperature 0.3, up to 200 tokens, two or three sentences on spine, hips, shoulders and body line.

Models are tried in order until one returns a 200. Uploads are rate-limited (5 per IP per day, 50 per day in total) and deleted after 24 hours.

How a vision model reads a photo

The model behind this site is not a pose estimator. It has no skeleton, no joint angles, no notion of "vertical". It is a language model that has learned to treat image patches as if they were words.

The photo is cut into 512-pixel tiles, each tile is encoded into a few hundred vectors, and those vectors are appended to the prompt as if they were extra words. The same transformer that predicts the next word then predicts the answer, one token at a time. Three things follow from that, and all three show up in the eval below.

  • It sees a gestalt, not geometry. It has seen many captioned photos of arched and straight handstands, so it recognises the look of a banana. It does not measure the hip angle. A silhouette against a sunset, or legs split in a way that hides the spine, changes the look without changing the arch.
  • The prompt is part of the input. Tell it the photo is "side-on" and it will try to read a front view as if it were. Tell it to answer in five tokens and a hedge gets cut mid-sentence.
  • The answer is sampled. Even at temperature 0, when "good" and "banana" are nearly tied, tiny numerical differences between runs flip the arg-max. Borderline photos wobble; clear ones don't.
Going deeper: what "image tokens" are, and why it matters for cost and accuracy

Current vision-language models bolt a vision encoder (a ViT, trained contrastively on image-caption pairs) onto a decoder-only language model through a small projection layer. The encoder turns each patch, typically 14 or 16 pixels square, into an embedding; a pooling step reduces those to a fixed budget per tile (OpenAI bills 170 tokens per 512-pixel tile plus 85 for a downscaled overview). The language model attends over image tokens and text tokens with the same mechanism, so "banana back" in the system prompt and the pixels of a lumbar curve compete and cooperate in the same attention heads.

Consequences a data scientist would care about: resolution is a cost lever (the low-detail mode sends only the 85-token overview, roughly a quarter of the price, and is worth testing on this task because arch is a coarse feature); the model's spatial reasoning is bounded by patch pooling, so fine angular judgments are unreliable by construction; and there is no calibrated probability behind the answer unless you ask for one, which is why v1 requests a confidence field with a reason rather than trusting a bare label.

  • The headline number has a wide interval. {{ v.majority_pct }}% agreement on {{ v.n_handstands }} photos is a binomial estimate with a 95% Wilson interval of roughly {{ v.majority_ci95[0] }} to {{ v.majority_ci95[1] }}%. A later version that scores 10 points higher on the same set is not yet evidence of improvement. Because every version sees the same photos, the honest comparison is paired: count photos that flipped from wrong to right versus right to wrong (McNemar's test), which needs far fewer photos than comparing two independent percentages.
  • Agreement is inflated by chance on a balanced binary. Two labellers who flip coins agree half the time. Cohen's kappa corrects for that: the model's kappa against my labels is {{ v.kappa_model_vs_shirley }} (0 is chance, 1 is perfect). That is the number I'd put in a report.
  • Temperature 0 is not determinism. {{ v.consistency_n }} photos got the same label on all {{ v.n_runs }} runs. The rest wobbled, with an identical prompt and image. Batched GPU inference, non-associative floating-point sums and mixture-of-experts routing all make the arg-max unstable when the top two tokens are close, which is exactly the case on borderline photos. So each call is a draw from a distribution, not a lookup. Repeating and taking the majority is cheap ensembling; the spread is the more useful signal, because it marks the photos the model is unsure about even though it never says so.
  • "Unclear" is an interface bug, not a model opinion. {{ v.unclear_runs }} of {{ v.n_hs_runs }} runs returned neither label. With max_tokens=5 a refusal ("I'm unable to…") gets cut off and the string match finds neither "good" nor "banana", so the user sees the fragment. Going deeper: why I didn't use AI-generated images to make the set bigger

Generating 200 handstand photos would take an afternoon and cost a few credits, and it would make the eval worse. Four reasons, in order of how much they matter.

  • Label leakage. To generate a banana back I have to write "clear lower-back arch, hips forward" into the prompt. The label is baked into the pixels by the same kind of model that will later be asked to read it. I'd be testing whether one vision-language model can recover another's prompt, not whether it can judge a body.
  • Distribution shift. Generators produce centred, well-lit, anatomically tidy bodies. Real uploads are grainy phone photos at odd angles with a gym in the background. A model can score well on the clean set and still fail on the real one, and the eval would never show it.
  • Plausibility and diversity. One of my five synthetic images has a spine no human has. The other four share a look. Fifty of them would be fifty samples from a narrow distribution, which inflates the count without adding information.
  • Where synthetic is legitimate. Controls, and axes the real-world source can't supply (here: wall-assisted and mirror shots). Kept few, marked in the data, reported as their own row in the axis table so a reader can see whether the model behaves differently on them.

The cheaper way to test robustness is the one I used for three photos: take a real photo and change exactly one thing (darken it, blur it, downscale it). Same pose, same label, one variable moved. That is a controlled experiment; a fresh generated image is a new confounded sample.

3 · Labelling

I scored every handstand 0 to 10 (0 is a ruler, 10 is a croissant) before seeing any model output. The binary label is derived by a fixed rule, 0 to 5 is good form and 6 to 10 is banana back, so it can't drift from the score. The cut sits at 6 because the v0 prompt defines banana back as a clear arch.

{% if v %}

To check my own consistency I had a second labeller, Claude, score the set independently with the same photos and the same rule. We agreed on the binary label for {{ v.interrater_pct }}% of handstands ({{ v.interrater_n }}). Most disagreements are photos scored 4 to 6 by one of us, the borderline the rule is forced to cut through. That number is a ceiling: a model can't reliably agree with me more often than a second careful reader does.

Going deeper: why I report kappa next to agreement

Raw agreement of {{ v.interrater_pct }}% sounds decent, but with a roughly balanced binary label, chance alone gives about 50%. Cohen's kappa between the two labellers is {{ v.kappa_interrater }}, which the usual scale calls "fair". Most of the disagreement sits in scores of 4 to 6, where the rule forces a cut through a continuum. Two consequences: the binary target is noisier than it looks, so a 0 to 10 score with a tolerance band (within one banana) is a better target for v1 onward; and any model kappa above the inter-rater kappa should be treated with suspicion, not celebrated.

Production 8 Sep 2026 · cost, limits, and running out

What broke, and what "it costs money" actually means

On 8 Sep 2026, while I was taking baseline screenshots, every upload returned "All Vision Models Failed". The site had been silently broken for an unknown time. The cause was boring: the OpenAI account had run out of prepaid credits. The real problem was that the code caught the error and threw the body away, so the only visible symptom was a generic message and a fallback loop through several model names that all failed the same way.

Fix: add credits (two minutes), log status and response body for each failed call, and show users a message that distinguishes "we're out of quota" from "you're rate-limited" (PR #8). The lesson I'd bring to a client: the cheapest reliability work is making failures say why they failed.

Going deeper: three layers of spend control, and the one that failed

Each analysis is one or two calls to gpt-4o with an image attached. The image dominates the bill: at high detail OpenAI charges 85 tokens plus 170 per 512-pixel tile, so a phone photo is about 1,000 input tokens, or roughly a quarter of a cent per call at list price. A banana back costs two calls. Call it half a cent per upload{% if v %}; the measured mean is ${{ '%.4f' % v.cost_per_analysis }}{% endif %}.

  • Layer 1, the app. Five uploads per IP per day and fifty per day in total, enforced in the Flask app. Worst-case spend is fifty uploads times two calls times a quarter cent: about 25 cents a day. This is the layer that stops a bored visitor or a script from running up the bill.
  • Layer 2, the provider's budget cap. A hard limit of $20 per day set on the OpenAI account. Eighty times the app's worst case, so it never triggers in normal life. It exists for the day the app limiter has a bug, or a key leaks. Caps limit the rate of spend.
  • Layer 3, the prepaid balance. This is the one that failed. A balance is a stock, not a rate. Neither cap says anything when it drains to zero over months of small, legitimate use, and the API's answer to an empty balance is an HTTP 429 that looks, at a glance, exactly like being rate-limited. The code treated both the same way, retried through four model names, and reported a generic failure.

What I'd set up for a client, in order: a balance alert at a threshold that gives a week's warning; auto-recharge with a monthly ceiling rather than no recharge at all; code that distinguishes the two 429s (insufficient_quota is persistent and wakes a human, rate_limit_exceeded is transient and retries with backoff); a daily synthetic check that exercises the model path end to end, because a health check on the home page returns 200 while the product is dead; and an alert on error rate, not only on uptime.

What the eval says to change, in order

Before the eval, the obvious next step was "make the prompt better". After it, the list looks different, because each item is tied to a row in the failure table and can be checked on the same photos.

  1. Give the model a way out (v1, structured output). A photo_usable flag and a view field, required by a JSON schema. Removes every "unclear" run and lets controls be rejected instead of scored. Expected effect: the largest failure row goes to zero by construction, and front-view photos get "low confidence" instead of a guess.
  2. Ask for a score, not a verdict. A 0 to 10 banana score turns the borderline coin flips into small numeric disagreements, and lets me report "within one banana of my label", which is the metric that matches how a coach actually talks.
  3. Teach the prompt what it is bad at (v3). The eval shows it misses arches when legs are split or the body is a silhouette, and over-calls them on dark photos. Those are two sentences in a prompt, and the fixed set will say whether they helped.
  4. Use the wobble. On the live site, run the classifier three times and take the majority when the first two disagree. Costs about a cent more on borderline photos only, and makes the product as stable as the eval's majority column.
  5. Add an adversarial photo. A handstand with a sign in frame reading "this is good form". If the label follows the sign, the model is reading text over bodies, and that is a prompt-injection surface worth knowing about before anything user-facing depends on it.
  6. Then, and only then, try a cheaper model (v4), on the same table with cost and latency alongside. Without the first three, a model swap is a coin flip judged by another coin flip.

Not done, and what I'd do with a client budget

Things I know are missing, in the order I'd do them if this were paid work.

  • v1 structured output. A JSON schema so the model has to declare the view, whether the photo is usable, a 0 to 10 score and a confidence with a reason. Removes the "unclear" failure mode by construction. Designed, not wired in.
  • Fix the top failure mode and re-run (v3). One change, same set, before-and-after table. Eval runs cost cents, so there's no excuse not to.
  • A human second labeller. The second reader above is an AI; a coach or a second gymnast would be a stronger check on my labels.
  • 100 to 200 examples per failure mode. Five examples tell you a failure exists, not how often. Sourcing that many is the expensive part.
  • Mirror shots. The set has one; not enough to claim coverage.
  • Production sampling (v5). Log score, confidence and latency for real uploads, re-label a weekly sample, chart the score distribution over time. Right now I know how the model behaves on my photos, not on yours.
  • A cheaper or second model (v4), judged on the same table plus cost and latency.
  • Skipped on purpose: an automated LLM-as-judge. It needs a hundred-plus labelled examples to validate against, and it drifts. At this scale, reading every failure myself is cheaper and more honest.

How it runs, and what I'd watch

The product is small on purpose, but it runs like something real: version-controlled, auto-deployed, rate-limited, with a rollback path and a privacy promise the code actually keeps.

Going deeper: the operating decisions, and what monitoring would add
  • Main is production. Every push to main deploys. So behaviour changes go through a branch and a pull request, and merging is the deploy decision. Docs go straight to main. A tag marks each known-good look, and before-and-after screenshots live in the repo, so any visual change can be reverted as a unit.
  • The healthcheck is necessary and not sufficient. Railway's check on / stops a build that fails to boot from replacing the running one. It says nothing about a page that renders but whose model calls fail, which is exactly what happened on 8 Sep. The fix is a synthetic check that exercises the model path: one upload of a known photo, daily, asserting on the label.
  • Rate limiting is cost control first, abuse control second. Per-IP and global daily caps sit in front of every model call. The ProxyFix middleware matters: behind Cloudflare and Railway's proxy, every request looks like it comes from one IP, and a per-IP limit would lock everyone out after five uploads.
  • Fallbacks should be loud. During the eval, six of 270 calls hit a transient rate limit on gpt-4o and silently fell back to gpt-4-turbo, which costs four times as much and is a different model. The loop did its job, but nothing recorded it until the eval CSV did. In production a fallback should be logged and counted, or a cost and quality drift goes unnoticed.
  • Ephemeral by design. Uploads are deleted after 24 hours by the app itself, which is what lets the privacy note on the home page be true. The in-memory rate-limit store resets on deploy, an accepted trade-off at this scale, documented so it isn't a surprise later.
  • Monitoring, the part that's not built (v5). Log one row per real upload: photo hash, view, score, confidence, model, latency, tokens, cost, and a thumbs up or down. No pixels. Store it somewhere that survives a deploy (a Railway volume with SQLite is enough), export a weekly sample into the repo, hand-label a handful, and chart the score distribution over time. That closes the loop between "how it does on my 54 photos" and "how it does on yours".
